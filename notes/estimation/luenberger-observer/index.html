Luenberger observer

Updated November 14, 2026

Suppose we want to control the velocity of a motor-driven cart. Our controller needs its current velocity to compare against the desired one, but our sensor measures only position. We could estimate velocity by subtracting consecutive position readings and dividing by the time between them. Unfortunately, this also amplifies measurement noise.

We also have a model. Given an estimated state and the input applied to the cart, it predicts how the cart will move. This gives us two complementary sources of information: the (imperfect) model which predicts motion, while sensor measurements reveal when that prediction is wrong. An observer combines them, into a better estimate than either can provide individually.

One might ask - how can a model be wrong? Indeed, the model for a cart is pretty simple. One easy example is if the moving cart is traversing rocky terrain, where wheels may slip and the acceleration applied to the wheels is hence not the acceleration that the cart would experience along the ground.

Let’s formalize the example. The controller sends an acceleration command uuu to the motor drive. We assume the drive produces the force F=muF=muF=mu, so v˙=u\dot v=uv˙=u. The sensor reports position, y=py=py=p. The observer receives both uuu and yyy, and estimates velocity for the controller.

Scalar Observer

Ignoring friction, our cart obeys

p˙=v,v˙=u,y=p.\dot p=v, \qquad \dot v=u, \qquad y=p.p˙​=v,v˙=u,y=p.

Position changes at the cart’s velocity, and velocity changes at the commanded acceleration. The observer runs a copy of these rules with its own estimates:

p^˙=v^,v^˙=u.\dot{\hat p}=\hat v, \qquad \dot{\hat v}=u.p^​˙​=v^,v^˙=u.

A hat means “our estimate of.” Knowing the acceleration tells us how velocity changes, but does not tell us its starting value. We must initialize v^\hat vv^ with a guess. Likewise, we initialize p^\hat pp^​, perhaps using the first position reading.

Suppose the cart is moving at 1 m/s1\,\mathrm{m/s}1m/s, but we initially guess that it is stationary. With u=0u=0u=0, the model keeps predicting zero velocity and a fixed position, while the real cart moves away.

Correcting position

The position sensor reports yyy, and our predicted sensor reading from the model is p^\hat pp^​. Their difference

r=y−p^r=y-\hat pr=y−p^​

is the residual (also known as the innovation). A positive residual means the cart is farther forward than we predicted. We can use it to correct the position estimate:

p^˙=v^+ℓpr.\dot{\hat p}=\hat v+\ell_p r.p^​˙​=v^+ℓp​r.

The model term v^\hat vv^ predicts motion and the correction ℓpr\ell_p rℓp​r pulls the estimate toward the measurement proportionally to the disagreement between them. The gain ℓp\ell_pℓp​ determines how strongly we respond.

Correcting position alone leaves our velocity guess unchanged. In our example, that guess is zero, so the position correction must keep dragging the estimate forward to follow the cart. To predict its motion correctly, we need to estimate velocity too. But what can a position residual actually tell us about velocity?

Correcting velocity

Recall that we have a position sensor that we can fuse with our position model to predict a better position than either could supply individually. However, in order for the model to be effective, we cannot assume constant velocity, since that is wrong. We should estimate it, but how do we do it with only a position sensor?

A single position residual cannot tell us whether our position estimate, velocity estimate, or both are wrong. If the cart is one meter ahead of our prediction, it might simply have started one meter farther forward. That measurement alone does not imply that it is moving faster than our model predicts.

The distinction appears over time. For a moment, consider the model running without measurement corrections, with u=0u=0u=0. Its position error is

p(t)−p^(t)=p(0)−p^(0)⏟initial position error+t (v(0)−v^(0))⏟initial velocity error.p(t)-\hat p(t) =\underbrace{p(0)-\hat p(0)}_{\text{initial position error}} +t\,\underbrace{\bigl(v(0)-\hat v(0)\bigr)}_{\text{initial velocity error}}.p(t)−p^​(t)=initial position errorp(0)−p^​(0)​​+tinitial velocity error(v(0)−v^(0))​​.

If the initial position is wrong but velocity is right, the offset stays constant. If velocity is wrong, the offset grows or shrinks. For example, starting one meter behind produces a constant one-meter discrepancy; underestimating velocity by 1 m/s1\,\mathrm{m/s}1m/s produces another meter of discrepancy every second. A sequence of position measurements can distinguish these cases even though one measurement cannot.

How do we use this information without directly differentiating noisy position readings? We let the position residual gradually adjust the velocity estimate as well:

v^˙=u+ℓvr.\dot{\hat v}=u+\ell_v r.v^˙=u+ℓv​r.

For a positive gain ℓv\ell_vℓv​, a positive residual nudges the velocity estimate upward. This is a provisional correction, not necessarily a conclusion that the velocity is wrong. The observer also corrects position, then compares its revised prediction against subsequent measurements. Its two estimates carry the effects of earlier readings forward, so the correction uses a history of measurements even though only the current residual appears explicitly in the equation.

If only the initial position was wrong, this rule can temporarily push the velocity estimate away from its true value, but later residuals undo that adjustment. With suitable gains, errors in both estimated position and velocity decay;

Also, note the units. We are correcting v^˙\dot{\hat v}v^˙, an acceleration, so ℓv\ell_vℓv​ has units of s−2\mathrm{s}^{-2}s−2. Multiplying it by the position residual in meters gives m/s2\mathrm{m/s^2}m/s2.

Together, the two rules form our Luenberger observer:

p^˙=v^+ℓp(y−p^),v^˙=u+ℓv(y−p^).\begin{aligned} \dot{\hat p}&=\hat v+\ell_p(y-\hat p),\\ \dot{\hat v}&=u+\ell_v(y-\hat p). \end{aligned}p^​˙​v^˙​=v^+ℓp​(y−p^​),=u+ℓv​(y−p^​).​

One residual now affects two estimates. How can we tell whether these corrections actually make the errors disappear? Define ep=p−p^e_p=p-\hat pep​=p−p^​ and ev=v−v^e_v=v-\hat vev​=v−v^. With an exact model and noiseless position measurements, subtracting the observer equations from the physical equations gives

e˙p=ev−ℓpep,e˙v=−ℓvep.\dot e_p=e_v-\ell_p e_p,\qquad \dot e_v=-\ell_v e_p.e˙p​=ev​−ℓp​ep​,e˙v​=−ℓv​ep​.

Error Dynamics

These equations let us check convergence to the true state directly. Let’s differentiate the position-error equation, then substitute the velocity-error equation:

e¨p=e˙v−ℓpe˙p=−ℓvep−ℓpe˙p.\ddot e_p=\dot e_v-\ell_p\dot e_p =-\ell_v e_p-\ell_p\dot e_p.e¨p​=e˙v​−ℓp​e˙p​=−ℓv​ep​−ℓp​e˙p​.

Rearrange it:

e¨p+ℓpe˙p+ℓvep=0.\boxed{\ddot e_p+\ell_p\dot e_p+\ell_v e_p=0.}e¨p​+ℓp​e˙p​+ℓv​ep​=0.​

This has the same form as a damped mass–spring system, with position error playing the role of spring displacement. We can verify the decay algebraically. As with the mass-spring damper, let’s try a solution ep(t)=ceste_p(t)=ce^{st}ep​(t)=cest. Substitution gives

s2+ℓps+ℓv=0,s1,2=−ℓp±ℓp2−4ℓv2.s^2+\ell_p s+\ell_v=0,\qquad s_{1,2}=\frac{-\ell_p\pm\sqrt{\ell_p^2-4\ell_v}}{2}.s2+ℓp​s+ℓv​=0,s1,2​=2−ℓp​±ℓp2​−4ℓv​​​.

When both gains are positive, both roots have negative real parts. If the square root is imaginary, their real part is −ℓp/2<0-\ell_p/2<0−ℓp​/2<0. If it is real, it is smaller than ℓp\ell_pℓp​, so even the root with the plus sign is negative. Thus every exponential mode decays.

For a concrete choice, let ℓp=5 s−1\ell_p=5\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}ℓp​=5s−1 and ℓv=6 s−2\ell_v=6\,\mathrm{s}^{-2}ℓv​=6s−2. The roots are −2-2−2 and −3 s−1-3\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}−3s−1, so, with time in seconds,

ep(t)=c1e−2t+c2e−3t.e_p(t)=c_1e^{-2t}+c_2e^{-3t}.ep​(t)=c1​e−2t+c2​e−3t.

The constants c1c_1c1​ and c2c_2c2​ depend on our initial errors. Whatever those constants are, both terms go to zero. This establishes position convergence, but we still need to check velocity. Rearrange the first error equation:

ev=e˙p+ℓpep.e_v=\dot e_p+\ell_p e_p.ev​=e˙p​+ℓp​ep​.

For our chosen gains, substituting the position-error solution gives

ev(t)=3c1e−2t+2c2e−3t,e_v(t)=3c_1e^{-2t}+2c_2e^{-3t},ev​(t)=3c1​e−2t+2c2​e−3t,

where the coefficients 3 and 2 have units of inverse seconds. Velocity error is made of the same decaying modes, so it also goes to zero. More generally, the decaying solutions above make both epe_pep​ and e˙p\dot e_pe˙p​ vanish, and hence make eve_vev​ vanish.

Measurement Noise

If the position sensor reports y=p+ny=p+ny=p+n, where nnn is noise, the error equations become

e˙p=ev−ℓpep−ℓpn,e˙v=−ℓvep−ℓvn.\dot e_p=e_v-\ell_p e_p-\ell_p n,\qquad \dot e_v=-\ell_v e_p-\ell_v n.e˙p​=ev​−ℓp​ep​−ℓp​n,e˙v​=−ℓv​ep​−ℓv​n.

Larger gains can speed up convergence, but also feed measurement noise more strongly into both estimates. We want errors to disappear at a useful rate without making the estimates too noisy, and how much noise we’re willing to accept in the estimate versus how quick the observer is to converge to the correct estimate as a tuning parameter.

For example, if the controller uses the estimate to control the vehicle, then an excessively noisy estimate will result in the vehicle motion being unacceptably jerky.

Matrix Form

Stacking the equations makes their structure visible:

x=[pv],A=[0100],B=[01],C=[10],L=[ℓpℓv].x=\begin{bmatrix}p\\v\end{bmatrix},\quad A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix},\quad B=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\end{bmatrix},\quad C=\begin{bmatrix}1&0\end{bmatrix},\quad L=\begin{bmatrix}\ell_p\\\ell_v\end{bmatrix}.x=[pv​],A=[00​10​],B=[01​],C=[1​0​],L=[ℓp​ℓv​​].

Then the system and observer become

x˙=Ax+Bu,y=Cx,x^˙=Ax^+Bu+L(y−Cx^).\dot x=Ax+Bu,\quad y=Cx,\qquad \boxed{\dot{\hat x}=A\hat x+Bu+L(y-C\hat x).}x˙=Ax+Bu,y=Cx,x^˙=Ax^+Bu+L(y−Cx^).​

AAA couples velocity into position. CCC selects the quantity our sensor sees. LLL distributes one sensor residual across two state corrections.

For nnn states and mmm sensor outputs, AAA is n×nn\times nn×n, CCC is m×nm\times nm×n, and LLL is n×mn\times mn×m. Each column of LLL describes how one sensor residual affects every state.

Choosing the error dynamics

Collect the errors into e=[ep,ev]⊤e=[e_p,e_v]^\tope=[ep​,ev​]⊤. With an exact model and noiseless measurements,

e˙=(A−LC)e.\dot e=(A-LC)e.e˙=(A−LC)e.

The eigenvalues of A−LCA-LCA−LC are the observer’s error poles. For the cart,

A−LC=[−ℓp1−ℓv0],det⁡(sI−(A−LC))=s2+ℓps+ℓv.A-LC=\begin{bmatrix}-\ell_p&1\\-\ell_v&0\end{bmatrix},\qquad \det\bigl(sI-(A-LC)\bigr)=s^2+\ell_p s+\ell_v.A−LC=[−ℓp​−ℓv​​10​],det(sI−(A−LC))=s2+ℓp​s+ℓv​.

To choose poles at −2-2−2 and −3 s−1-3\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}−3s−1, match this polynomial to (s+2)(s+3)=s2+5s+6(s+2)(s+3)=s^2+5s+6(s+2)(s+3)=s2+5s+6. Thus ℓp=5 s−1\ell_p=5\,\mathrm{s}^{-1}ℓp​=5s−1 and ℓv=6 s−2\ell_v=6\,\mathrm{s}^{-2}ℓv​=6s−2. Both position and velocity errors decay, even though velocity is never directly measured.

Observability and Detectability

An observer cannot recover arbitrary hidden parts of the state (like velocity in our example) just because we add them to a vector. The measurements over time must contain information about them.

For the cart with no acceleration, p(t)=p(0)+tv(0)p(t)=p(0)+tv(0)p(t)=p(0)+tv(0). Two noiseless position readings at distinct times determine both the initial position and velocity. The system is hence observable, meaning that every part of the state leaves a ‘trace’ in the sensor reading. We form the following matrix to test for Observability:

O=[CCA]=[1001].\mathcal O=\begin{bmatrix}C\\CA\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}.O=[CCA​]=[10​01​].

Its rank is two, so neither state direction is hidden. In an nnn-state linear time-invariant system, stack C,CA,…,CAn−1C,CA,\ldots,CA^{n-1}C,CA,…,CAn−1 and check for rank nnn. Observability permits arbitrary observer pole placement.

If we measured only velocity, C=[01]C=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\end{bmatrix}C=[0​1​], adding a constant to the cart’s position would never change any reading. Absolute position is unobservable, and its error cannot be corrected by this sensor. More generally, a convergent observer needs detectability: any unobservable modes must already decay on their own.

How to choose a gain?

The Luenberger observer gives us a reusable structure - predict from a model, compare predicted and actual measurements, and feed the residual back into the estimate. We choose LLL to get useful error dynamics. This is a powerful structure, but the gain we choose and the observer dynamics implied by it are somewhat arbitrary - we have no notion of whether the estimate we produce is optimal.

In the next note, we will explore the Kalman Filter, which defines what optimal means for our observer and how we can automatically compute the optimal gain for our observer.
