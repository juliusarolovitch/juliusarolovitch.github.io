Reward functions

Updated October 31, 2026

Introduction

Given an MDP M=(S,A,P,R,ρ0,γ)\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \rho_0, \gamma)M=(S,A,P,R,ρ0​,γ) - representing state space, action space, transition kernel, reward function, initial state distribution, and discount factor - and the standard objective J(π)=Eπ[∑tγtRt]J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\sum_t \gamma^t R_t]J(π)=Eπ​[∑t​γtRt​], we design reward functions to shape the behaviour of a policy. Reward function failure modes can be largely grouped into two:

  1. Misspecification, whereby the reward’s optimum is not what we wanted. The agent succeeds at the stated problem but the reward didn’t represent the desired behavior.
  2. Uninformativeness, whereby the reward’s optimum is correct but unreachable by the optimizer. The reward carries too little gradient to guide the search, classically because it is too sparse.

The obvious cure for an uninformative reward is to add dense intermediate rewards for behaviours we deem useful, though each such addition adds the risk of misspecification whereby the reward now rewards the hypothesis about good behavior rather than the desired outcome. The obvious cure for misspecification is to reward only the true outcome, which is typically sparse. This is the primary tradeoff in reward design.

Basic Transformations of a Reward

Reinforcement learning rests on the premise that goals and purposes can be adequately represented in a single, scalar signal through the sum of individual terms. Writing R=∑iwiRiR = \sum_i w_iR_iR=∑i​wi​Ri​ specifies a tradeoff between individual objectives. The optimizer will find and exploit that exchange rate at the extremes, where our intuitions about the weights were never tested.

Safe Transformations

  • Positive Scaling - for α>0\alpha > 0α>0, replacing RRR with αR\alpha RαR does not change the optimal policy set. Hence, the absolute reward magnitudes do not matter but relative magnitudes between terms do. One caveat, however, is that this invariance only holds for the unregularized objective. With a fixed entropy coefficient or temperature, scaling RRR changes the optimal policy.

  • Constant Offsets - while replacing RRR with R+bR+bR+b may seem innocent, this is only the case in the infinite-horizon case. But in the episodic setting with a variable termination time TTT, the shift is:

    ∑t=0T−1γtb=b(1+γ+γ2+⋯+γT−1)=b 1−γT1−γ\sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \gamma^t b = b\left(1+\gamma+\gamma^2+\cdots+\gamma^{T-1}\right) = b\,\frac{1-\gamma^T}{1-\gamma}t=0∑T−1​γtb=b(1+γ+γ2+⋯+γT−1)=b1−γ1−γT​

Potential-Based Reward Shaping

Potential-based reward shaping involves assigning each state a score and paying the agent the change in that score as it moves. This is an alternative to rewarding the action itself. The danger comes from a potential function that creates a cycle in the state space which the agent can farm without achieving the goal itself. Any cycle in the state space must have a net reward of zero to ensure this is impossible. Another important caveat is that adding correct potential-based reward shaping does not change the optimal policy set, it simply changes (shapes) how easy it is for the optimizer to discover it.

Suppose we augment RRR with an additional shaping term F(s,a,s′)F(s, a, s')F(s,a,s′), producing M′=(S,A,P,R+F,ρ0,γ)\mathcal{M}' = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R + F, \rho_0, \gamma)M′=(S,A,P,R+F,ρ0​,γ). The shaping term is governed by the potential function Φ(s)\Phi(s)Φ(s). When is the optimal policy unchanged?

Theorem: Let Φ:S→R\Phi : \mathcal{S} \to \RΦ:S→R be any bounded function and define F(s,a,s′)=γΦ(s′)−Φ(s).F(s, a, s') = \gamma \Phi(s') - \Phi(s).F(s,a,s′)=γΦ(s′)−Φ(s). Then every optimal policy of M′\mathcal{M}'M′ is optimal in M\mathcal{M}M and vice versa. Moreover, the optimal action-value functions are related by:

QM′∗(s,a)=QM∗(s,a)−Φ(s).Q^*_{\mathcal{M}'}(s, a) = Q^*_{\mathcal{M}}(s, a) - \Phi(s).QM′∗​(s,a)=QM∗​(s,a)−Φ(s).

The Informativeness Axis

The Cost of Sparsity

Let’s consider the canonical hard case - a deterministic MDP of horizon HHH with ∣A∣\vert \mathcal{A}\vert∣A∣ actions per state, where reward 1 is delivered only for one specific action sequence and 0 everywhere else. A uniform random policy reaches the reward with probability P(success)=∣A∣−H\mathbb{P}(\text{success})=\vert \mathcal{A}\vert^{-H}P(success)=∣A∣−H. Until then, the gradient is zero and there is hence nothing to learn from. Sparse-reward problems are hence exploration problems and are not an amenable setup to the optimizer for large horizons and action spaces.

This is why the true task metric is often unusable as a training signal, while it may be the most faithful specification possible. The difficulty of credit assignment to states scales with the effective horizon 11−γ\frac{1}{1-\gamma}1−γ1​ - the number of steps over which the reward must be propagated backwards. A potential Φ=V∗\Phi=V^*Φ=V∗ pre-propagates that information and reduces the effective horizon towards 1. Even a Φ\PhiΦ capturing the coarse structure of V∗V^*V∗ shortens the horizon over which the optimizer must discover the rest.

Interaction of Dense Rewards with Terminal Rewards

A dense per-step term of magnitude ccc contributes c1−γ\frac{c}{1-\gamma}1−γc​ to the return. This is seen by expanding the rewards over time - the dense reward term contributes the same amount ccc at each step: c+γc+γ2c+γ3c+⋯c+\gamma c+\gamma^2c+\gamma^3c+\cdotsc+γc+γ2c+γ3c+⋯. When we factor out ccc, we find c(1+γ+γ2+γ3+⋯ )c\left(1+\gamma+\gamma^2+\gamma^3+\cdots\right)c(1+γ+γ2+γ3+⋯). The term inside the parentheses is an infinite geometric series:

1+γ+γ2+γ3+⋯=11−γ,0≤γ<1.1+\gamma+\gamma^2+\gamma^3+\cdots=\frac{1}{1-\gamma},\qquad 0\leq\gamma<1.1+γ+γ2+γ3+⋯=1−γ1​,0≤γ<1.

Hence, the overall dense term amounts to c1−γ\frac{c}{1-\gamma}1−γc​. When we provide a terminal event reward of magnitude RtermR_{term}Rterm​, we must compare its magnitude to the magnitude of the accumulated dense per-step reward to provide a meaningful gradient towards the desired terminal state.

Misspecification and Reward Hacking

Hackable Rewards

Let R⋆R^{\star}R⋆ be the true objective and R~\tilde{R}R~ the proxy we optimize. Informally, reward hacking occurs when increasing JR~J_{\tilde{R}}JR~​ decreases JR⋆J_{R^{\star}}JR⋆​. An objective pair (R∗,R~)(R^*, \tilde{R})(R∗,R~) is deemed unhackable if no policy improvement under the proxy ever corresponds to a regression under the true reward. Formally, an objective pair is unhackable iff:

  1. The ordering of rewards over all stochastic policies between any two states is equivalent by both objectives
  2. The ordering of rewards over all stochastic policies between any two states is trivial by at least one of the two objectives - i.e. the objective assigns the same return to all policies between the same two states

Only in these two cases will an improvement in the proxy never regress the true reward.

The second point is worth explaining more closely. One example of a trivial return is an objective that provides a reward of 1 at each step over episodes of TTT timesteps. Then, if we know any two policies started at state S0S_0S0​ and reached state SGS_GSG​ both in TTT timesteps, then their returns were equal no matter the intermediate trajectories. Another example is potential-based terms whose return over a trajectory between two states SS,SGS_S, S_GSS​,SG​ is simply γΦ(SG)−Φ(SS)\gamma\Phi(S_G)-\Phi(S_S)γΦ(SG​)−Φ(SS​) - once again, the intermediate trajectory does not matter for the cumulative return.

Hence, when shaping the reward we strive to construct a proxy for the true objective that does not admit a different ordering of policies in their return between the same start and goal states. Any proxy that induces a genuinely different ordering over policies - not merely a different numerical reward - admits some policy pair on which optimizing the proxy makes the true objective worse.

Multiplicative Rewards

Another useful idea is as follows - the default reward is a weighted sum R=∑iwiRiR = \sum_i w_i R_iR=∑i​wi​Ri​. Its defining property is substitutability whereby the agent may abandon one term entirely if the resulting gain elsewhere exceeds the loss. When terms are conjunctive requirements - do the task and stay smooth and respect the limit - substitutability is wrong. The alternative is multiplicative composition:

R=∏iRi,so per steplog⁡R=∑ilog⁡Ri.R = \prod_i R_i, \qquad\text{so per step}\qquad \log R = \sum_i \log R_i .R=i∏​Ri​,so per steplogR=i∑​logRi​.

With each Ri∈(0,1]R_i \in (0,1]Ri​∈(0,1]. The cost for this is a bootstrapping problem - early in training, when some factor is near zero, the whole product is near zero everywhere and the gradient signal for the other factors is crushed. Multiplicative rewards therefore pair well with curricula or with hybrids in which the task terms multiply and the regularization terms are added.

Summary

During training, a couple points are useful in practice:

  1. Maintain a true metric you don’t optimize over and compare it to the proxy reward. Divergence between the two is indicative of hacking.
    1. Example: consider a warehouse robot with a proxy reward of +1 per box moved while the real objective is to move unique boxes to the truck. The agent might discover that if it picks up one box, puts it down, and picks it up again then the proxy reward skyrockets. Meanwhile, the intended objective isn’t being completed and the true objective stays at 0.
  2. Reward misspecification is more likely to surface later in training as optimizer capability improves. Rewards should be audited anytime capability is changed - e.g. more training time, larger NN, etc.
    1. Example: consider a robot tasked with cleaning a room where its reward is volume of dirt collected into the dust pan. Early on in training the robot performs poorly and cleans only 10% of the room. It soon improves and cleans 90% of the room. Optimization pressure increases and the robot then finds that it can exploit rewards by dumping the dustpan onto the floor and then collecting the dirt back into it. The reward bug was always present but was not found by the weaker, earlier-stage policy.
      1. The point here is that a more capable optimizer, either by means of having converged to a good policy or being run with greater capacity than previous runs, will be more likely to find loopholes in the reward. This does not mean that the loopholes were never there. When we increase capability, we must audit for reward hacking.
  3. When balancing multiple desired aspects of the behavior (e.g. maintaining altitude, minimizing control effort, maintaining airspeed) - bias towards including all of them in a reward rather than a subset that still appears to produce reasonable behavior. Sometimes we may see nominal behavior while only representing a subset of the desired behaviors in the reward, but the optimizer will optimize non-included variables to its desire to maximize rewards for the included ones. Hence, even an arbitrarily-weighted composition of all desired terms is safer than a tuned subset that appears to function well.

Constrained MDPs

When a requirement is a constraint - such as staying above an airspeed, keeping roll bounded - encoding it as a penalty term forces the optimizer to discover by trial and error the weight at which the constraint happens to bind. That weight depends on the reward scale, the discount, and the current policy, so it does not transfer between tasks. The principal alternative is a constrained MDP.

Typically, we solve max⁡πJR(π)\max_\pi J_R(\pi)maxπ​JR​(π), where JR(π)=Eπ[∑t=0∞γtRt]J_R(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_\pi\left[\sum_{t=0}^\infty \gamma^t R_t\right]JR​(π)=Eπ​[∑t=0∞​γtRt​] - the expected discounted return of the reward. Now suppose we’re flying a drone and one of the constraints is to keep current below 70A. We can define another signal, c(s,a,s′)c(s,a,s')c(s,a,s′), denoted cost. We can then define the discounted accumulated cost as Jc(π)=Eπ[∑tγtct]J_c(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_\pi\left[\sum_t \gamma^t c_t\right]Jc​(π)=Eπ​[∑t​γtct​]. We also specify the maximum cost we’re willing to tolerate ddd.

A constrained MDP can be formulated as:

max⁡π  JR(π)subject toJc(π)≤d,\max_{\pi} \; J_{R}(\pi) \qquad \text{subject to} \qquad J_c(\pi) \le d,πmax​JR​(π)subject toJc​(π)≤d,

L(π,λ)=JR(π)−λ(Jc(π)−d),λ≥0,\mathcal{L}(\pi, \lambda) = J_{R}(\pi) - \lambda \big( J_c(\pi) - d \big), \qquad \lambda \ge 0,L(π,λ)=JR​(π)−λ(Jc​(π)−d),λ≥0,

λ  ←  [λ+ηλ(J^c(π)−d)]+.\lambda \;\leftarrow\; \big[ \lambda + \eta_\lambda \big( \hat{J}_c(\pi) - d \big) \big]_{+}.λ←[λ+ηλ​(J^c​(π)−d)]+​.

Since the primal problem is a maximization, the dual function g(λ)=max⁡πL(π,λ)g(\lambda) = \max_\pi \mathcal{L}(\pi,\lambda)g(λ)=maxπ​L(π,λ) is minimized. The multiplier λ\lambdaλ is the penalty weight we would’ve otherwise tuned by hand but the update formulation tunes it automatically, increasing it while the constraint is violated and relaxing it once satisfied.
