This page collects useful definitions and terms commonly used in reinforcement learning literature, written with assistance from Claude.

## 1. The problem

### Markov decision process

A **Markov decision process** (MDP) is the tuple $\mathcal{M} = (\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \rho_0, \gamma)$:

- $\mathcal{S}$ is the **state space**.
  A state $s \in \mathcal{S}$ is everything the agent needs to know about the world to predict what happens next.
- $\mathcal{A}$ is the **action space**.
  An action $a \in \mathcal{A}$ is what the agent chooses.
  It is **discrete** if it is a finite set (left, right, fire) and **continuous** if it is a subset of $\mathbb{R}^m$ (motor torques).
- $P(s' \mid s, a)$ is the **transition kernel**: the probability (or density) of landing in $s'$ after taking $a$ in $s$.
- $R(s, a, s')$ is the **reward function**.
  It returns a scalar.
  We write $R_t$ for the reward received at step $t$.
- $\rho_0(s)$ is the **initial state distribution**.
- $\gamma \in [0, 1)$ is the **discount factor**.
  It makes a reward $k$ steps in the future worth $\gamma^k$ times a reward now.

<Aka>

The tuple is often shortened to $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, R, \gamma)$.
The transition kernel is also written $T(s' \mid s, a)$, $p(s' \mid s, a)$ or $\mathcal{P}^a_{ss'}$,
and called the **dynamics** or **transition function**.
The reward function is also written $r(s, a)$ or $\mathcal{R}(s, a)$, often without $s'$.
The initial distribution is also $\mu$, $d_0$ or $p_0$.
In control texts, the state is $x$ and the action is $u$,
and reward is replaced by a **cost** $c$ or $\ell$ to be minimized.

</Aka>

The word **Markov** means that $P$ and $R$ depend only on the current $s$ and $a$, not on the history before them.
If the agent only sees an observation $o$ that does not contain the full state,
the problem is a **partially observable Markov decision process** (POMDP).


### Steps, episodes, trajectories

At each **time step** $t$, the agent sees $s_t$, picks $a_t$,
and the environment returns $R_t$ and $s_{t+1} \sim P(\cdot \mid s_t, a_t)$.

A **trajectory** is the sequence

$$
\tau = (s_0, a_0, R_0, s_1, a_1, R_1, \ldots).
$$

<Aka>

Rollout, path, or (for a complete one) episode.

</Aka>

An **episode** is a trajectory that ends at a **terminal state**, after $T$ steps.
Tasks with episodes are **episodic**.
Tasks that never end are **continuing**.

<Aka>

$T$ is also written $H$ and called the **horizon**.
A finite-horizon Markov decision process has a fixed $H$ and often no discount.

</Aka>

Episodes typically end for one of two reasons:
- **Termination**: the Markov decision process itself reaches a terminal state (the robot fell over, the goal was reached).
  No future reward exists after this state.
- **Truncation**: we stopped the episode for some reason, such as a time limit.
  The Markov decision process did not end, so future reward still exists.

<Aka>

Papers usually write a termination flag $d_t$ or $\text{done}_t$, equal to $1$ at a terminal state.

</Aka>

### Reward, return and value

Three quantities are easy to confuse:

- The **reward** $R_t$ is the signal from **one** step. It says how good the last transition was.
- The **return** $G_t$ is the discounted sum of all rewards from step $t$ on, in one particular trajectory.
  It is a random variable.
- The **value** $V^\pi(s)$ is the **expected** return.
  It averages the return over every way the future can unfold.
  (and is defined in the next section)

The return is

$$
G_t = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \gamma^k R_{t+k} = R_t + \gamma G_{t+1}.
$$

The second form is the recursion that most of reinforcement learning is built on.
The **effective horizon** is about $1/(1-\gamma)$ steps.

<Aka>

The return is written $G_t$ (Sutton and Barto), $R_t$ (older papers, where it clashes with the reward),
$R(\tau)$ for a whole trajectory, and $\hat R_t$ when it is an estimate.
It is also called the **reward-to-go**, **cumulative reward** or **discounted return**.
The undiscounted sum is the **episode return**, which is what training curves usually plot,
even when the algorithm optimizes the discounted one.

</Aka>

### Policy

A **policy** chooses actions.

- A **stochastic policy** $\pi(a \mid s)$ is a probability distribution over actions for each state.
- A **deterministic policy** $\mu(s)$ returns one action.
- A **parameterized policy** $\pi_\theta$ has parameters $\theta$, usually the weights of a neural network.
  For discrete actions the network outputs logits and a softmax turns them into probabilities.
  For continuous actions it usually outputs the mean $\mu_\theta(s)$ and standard deviation $\sigma_\theta(s)$ of a Gaussian.

<Aka>

$\pi(s, a)$ or $\pi(a, s)$ in older texts.
A deterministic policy is also written $\pi(s)$ or $\mu_\theta(s)$, which clashes with the Gaussian mean.
The policy network is the **actor**.

</Aka>

### Objective

The **objective** is the expected return from the start:

$$
J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi}\big[G_0\big],
$$

where $\tau \sim \pi$ means $s_0 \sim \rho_0$, $a_t \sim \pi(\cdot \mid s_t)$ and $s_{t+1} \sim P(\cdot \mid s_t, a_t)$.
An **optimal policy** $\pi^*$ maximizes $J$.

<Aka>

$J(\theta)$ or $J(\pi_\theta)$ for a parameterized policy, $\eta(\pi)$ (the trust region policy optimization paper),
and **expected return** or **performance**.
Since $J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_{s_0 \sim \rho_0}[V^\pi(s_0)]$,
some texts write it as $V^\pi(\rho_0)$ or $v_{\pi_\theta}(s_0)$.

</Aka>

## 2. Value functions

### State value and action value

The **state-value function** is the expected return when we start in $s$ and follow $\pi$:

$$
V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\big[G_t \mid s_t = s\big].
$$

The **action-value function** (or **Q-function**) is the expected return when we start in $s$,
take $a$ first, and follow $\pi$ after:

$$
Q^\pi(s, a) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\big[G_t \mid s_t = s,\, a_t = a\big].
$$

They are related by averaging over the policy's action:
$V^\pi(s) = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi(\cdot \mid s)}\big[Q^\pi(s, a)\big]$.

<Aka>

$v_\pi(s)$ and $q_\pi(s, a)$ (Sutton and Barto), $V_\pi$ and $Q_\pi$, or $V(s)$ and $Q(s, a)$ when the policy is clear.
The learned approximations are $V_\phi$, $\hat v(s, \mathbf{w})$ (Sutton and Barto), $Q_\theta$ or $Q(s, a; \theta)$.
The state value is often just called **the value**, and $Q$ the **state-action value** or **quality**.
A network that learns either one is a **critic**, and in proximal policy optimization code it is the **value head**.

</Aka>

### Advantage

The **advantage** says how much better $a$ is than the policy's average action in $s$:

$$
A^\pi(s, a) = Q^\pi(s, a) - V^\pi(s).
$$

By definition, $\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi}\big[A^\pi(s, a)\big] = 0$.
A positive advantage means "do this more often", a negative one "do this less often".
This is the quantity a policy gradient needs.


### Optimal values

$V^*(s) = \max_\pi V^\pi(s)$ and $Q^*(s, a) = \max_\pi Q^\pi(s, a)$.
If we know $Q^*$, the greedy policy $\pi^*(s) = \arg\max_a Q^*(s, a)$ is optimal.

<Aka>

$v_*$ and $q_*$ (Sutton and Barto).

</Aka>

### Bellman equations

Substituting $G_t = R_t + \gamma G_{t+1}$ into the definitions gives the **Bellman expectation equations**:

$$
\begin{aligned}
V^\pi(s) &= \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi,\, s' \sim P}\big[R + \gamma V^\pi(s')\big],\\
Q^\pi(s, a) &= \mathbb{E}_{s' \sim P}\Big[R + \gamma\, \mathbb{E}_{a' \sim \pi(\cdot \mid s')}\big[Q^\pi(s', a')\big]\Big].
\end{aligned}
$$

For the optimal values, the average over $\pi$ is replaced by a max.
These are the **Bellman optimality equations**:

$$
Q^*(s, a) = \mathbb{E}_{s' \sim P}\Big[R + \gamma \max_{a'} Q^*(s', a')\Big].
$$

Notice that for the $Q^\pi$ equation, we only need a transition $(s, a, R, s')$ from the environment and a sample $a' \sim \pi(\cdot \mid s')$, both of which we can draw ourselves.
The transition can come from any policy. This is why Q-learning can be done off-policy (defined below) and the quality of the learned function scales how representative the samples are.

### Discounted state distribution

$d^\pi(s)$ is the **discounted state-visitation distribution**, which represents how often $\pi$ visits $s$,
with later visits weighted down by $\gamma^t$:

$$
d^\pi(s) = (1-\gamma) \sum_{t=0}^{\infty} \gamma^t \Pr(s_t = s \mid \pi).
$$

The factor $1-\gamma$ makes it sum to one, and we sample from it by running the policy and counting the states it visits.

<Aka>

$\rho^\pi$ or $\rho_\pi$ (the trust region policy optimization and soft actor-critic papers, often without the $1-\gamma$ factor),
$\mu(s)$ (Sutton and Barto, the on-policy distribution), **occupancy measure** (often over state-action pairs,
$d^\pi(s, a)$), or **state marginal**.

</Aka>

## 3. Learning value functions

### Bootstrapping and targets

To fit an estimate $V_\phi$ (a network with parameters $\phi$), we regress it onto a **target**.
Two kinds of target exist:

- **Monte Carlo** (MC): use the observed return $G_t$.
  It is **unbiased** but has **high variance**, since it adds up the randomness of every future step.
- **Temporal difference** (TD): use $R_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1})$.
  It uses our own estimate of the future in place of the real future.
  This is called **bootstrapping**.
  It has **low variance** but it is **biased** while $V_\phi$ is wrong.

### Temporal-difference error

The **temporal-difference error** is used to evaluate the consistency of the learned value function and is expressed as:

$$
\delta_t = R_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t).
$$

If $V = V^\pi$, then $\mathbb{E}[\delta_t \mid s_t, a_t] = A^\pi(s_t, a_t)$.
So $\delta_t$ is a one-step, one-sample estimate of the advantage.

<Aka>

**temporal-difference residual** (the generalized advantage estimation paper, written $\delta_t^V$),
**one-step advantage**, or Bellman error of a single sample.

</Aka>

### $n$-step returns

Between the two extremes, the **$n$-step return** uses $n$ real rewards and then bootstraps:

$$
G_t^{(n)} = R_t + \gamma R_{t+1} + \cdots + \gamma^{n-1} R_{t+n-1} + \gamma^n V(s_{t+n}).
$$

$n = 1$ is the temporal-difference target and $n = \infty$ is the Monte Carlo target.
Larger $n$ trades bias for variance.

<Aka>

$G_{t:t+n}$ (Sutton and Barto), $R_t^{(n)}$, or **multi-step return**.
The exponentially weighted average of all $n$-step returns is the **$\lambda$-return** $G_t^\lambda$,
used by temporal-difference learning with eligibility traces.

</Aka>

### Target networks

A **target network** $\bar\phi$ is a slow copy of $\phi$ used only inside the target $R + \gamma Q_{\bar\phi}(s', a')$.
Without it, every gradient step moves the target it chases.
It is updated either by periodic copying or by **Polyak averaging**,
$\bar\phi \leftarrow \tau \phi + (1-\tau)\bar\phi$ with small $\tau$ such as $0.005$.

### Overestimation bias

If $Q$ estimates are noisy, then $\max_a Q(s, a)$ is biased upward, since the max picks whichever action happened to have the largest positive error.
Because the target bootstraps on this max, the bias compounds through the Bellman recursion.

### The deadly triad

Function approximation, bootstrapping and off-policy data are each useful.
Combined, they can make value learning diverge.
Target networks, double Q-learning and conservative updates exist to keep this combination stable.

## 4. Families of algorithms

### Model-free and model-based

A **model-based** method learns or is given $P$ and $R$, and plans or generates synthetic data with them.
A **model-free** method learns a policy or value function directly from transitions without a model.
Proximal policy optimization and soft actor-critic are both model-free.

<Aka>

A learned model is a **world model** or **dynamics model**, written $\hat P$, $\hat T$ or $f_\psi$.

</Aka>

### Value-based, policy-based, actor-critic

- **Value-based** methods learn $Q$ and act greedily with respect to it (deep Q-networks, or DQN).
  They suit discrete actions, because $\arg\max_a$ over a continuous space is itself an optimization.
- **Policy-based** methods (or **policy gradient** methods) adjust $\pi_\theta$ directly in the direction that increases $J$ (REINFORCE).
- **Actor-critic** methods do both.
  The **actor** is the policy $\pi_\theta$, while the **critic** is a learned value function $V_\phi$ or $Q_\phi$ that reduces the variance of the actor's update.
  Proximal policy optimization and soft actor-critic are both actor-critic.

<Aka>

Value-based methods are also called **Q-learning methods** or, for exact tabular versions,
**dynamic programming** (value iteration, policy iteration).
Policy-based methods are also called **policy search** or **policy optimization**.

</Aka>

### Policy gradient

The **policy gradient theorem** gives the gradient of $J$ without differentiating through the environment:

$$
\nabla_\theta J(\pi_\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi_\theta}\left[\sum_{t} \gamma^t\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, A^{\pi_\theta}(s_t, a_t)\right].
$$

The term $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ is the **score function**.
It is the direction in parameter space that makes $a$ more likely in $s$.
The gradient pushes up the log-probability of actions with positive advantage and pushes down those with negative advantage.
In practice, the $\gamma^t$ factor is usually dropped, which gives a slightly biased but better-behaved estimator.

The key identity behind it is the **log-derivative trick**:
$\nabla_\theta p_\theta(x) = p_\theta(x) \nabla_\theta \log p_\theta(x)$.
It turns the gradient of an expectation into an expectation we can sample.


### Reparameterization gradient

A second way to differentiate an expectation over actions exists when the action can be written as a differentiable function of noise:
$a = f_\theta(s, \xi)$ with $\xi \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I)$.
Then

$$
\nabla_\theta\, \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta}\big[Q(s, a)\big] = \mathbb{E}_{\xi}\big[\nabla_a Q(s, a)\, \nabla_\theta f_\theta(s, \xi)\big].
$$

This uses the gradient of $Q$ with respect to the action, so it needs a differentiable critic and continuous actions.
It usually has much lower variance than the score-function form.
Soft actor-critic uses it.

<Aka>

The **reparameterization trick**, the **pathwise derivative**.
The noise is written $\xi$, $\epsilon$ (the soft actor-critic paper) or $\varepsilon$.
The deterministic special case, where $a = \mu_\theta(s)$ and there is no noise,
is the **deterministic policy gradient**.

</Aka>

## 5. Training

### Behavior and target policy

The **behavior policy** $\beta$ is the policy that collected the data.
The **target policy** $\pi$ is the policy we are evaluating or improving.

<Aka>

The behavior policy is written $b$ (Sutton and Barto), $\mu$ or $\pi_\beta$.
It is also called the **data-collection**, **logging** or **rollout** policy.
"Target policy" has nothing to do with target networks.

</Aka>

### On-policy

An **on-policy** method uses data only from the current policy:
$\beta = \pi$.
After each update the old data no longer comes from the current policy, so it is thrown away.
On-policy methods (REINFORCE, advantage actor-critic, trust region policy optimization, proximal policy optimization) are usually stable and simple to reason about,
but **sample inefficient** since each transition is used for only a few gradient steps.

### Off-policy

An **off-policy** method can learn about $\pi$ from data collected by any $\beta$,
including old versions of $\pi$, other agents, or humans.
It stores transitions in a **replay buffer** $\mathcal{D}$ and samples minibatches from it many times.
Off-policy methods (deep Q-networks, soft actor-critic) are much more **sample efficient**,
but more sensitive to hyperparameters and to the deadly triad.

What makes a method off-policy is the form of its update.
Q-function targets (see the Bellman equation above) only need $(s, a, R, s')$ and a fresh $a'$ from the current policy,
so they are valid on any transition.
The policy gradient is an expectation over states and actions *from $\pi$*, so it is on-policy unless corrected.

<Aka>

The replay buffer is also written $\mathcal{B}$ or $\mathcal{R}$, and called **experience replay** or **replay memory**.
A transition $(s, a, R, s', d)$ is also called an **experience** or **sample**,
and the tuple is often written $(s, a, r, s')$.

</Aka>

### Importance sampling

**Importance sampling** reweights samples from one distribution to estimate an expectation under another:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\big[f(x)\big] = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim q}\left[\frac{p(x)}{q(x)} f(x)\right].
$$

For actions, the **importance ratio** is $\pi(a \mid s) / \beta(a \mid s)$.
It is unbiased but its variance grows fast as $\pi$ and $\beta$ separate, and ratios over many steps multiply.
Proximal policy optimization uses a single-step ratio and keeps $\pi$ close to $\beta$ so the ratio stays near one.

### Online and offline

**Online** reinforcement learning interacts with the environment while it learns.
**Offline** reinforcement learning learns from a fixed dataset and never interacts.
Offline reinforcement learning is off-policy by necessity,
with the extra problem that it can never collect data to check actions the dataset does not contain.


### Training Efficiency

**Sample efficiency** counts environment steps needed to reach a given performance.
It matters most when steps are expensive, such as on a real robot.
When a fast simulator can run thousands of environments in parallel, wall-clock time often matters more,
and on-policy methods can be competitive or better.

## 6. Exploration

### Exploration and exploitation

**Exploitation** picks the action currently believed best.
**Exploration** picks other actions to learn whether they are better.
Too little exploration converges to a poor local optimum that may not generalize well, which too much exploration is sample inefficient.

### Common methods

- **$\epsilon$-greedy**: with probability $\epsilon$ take a random action, otherwise the greedy one.
  Used with discrete value-based methods.
- **Stochastic policies**: sampling from $\pi_\theta$ explores by itself.
  Its randomness shrinks as training sharpens the policy, sometimes too early.
- **Entropy regularization**: add a bonus for policy randomness to the objective,
  so the policy stays stochastic until there is a reason to commit.

### Entropy

The **entropy** of a policy at state $s$ measures its randomness:

$$
\mathcal{H}\big(\pi(\cdot \mid s)\big) = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi(\cdot \mid s)}\big[-\log \pi(a \mid s)\big].
$$

For $n$ discrete actions it ranges from $0$ (deterministic) to $\log n$ (uniform).
For continuous actions it is a **differential entropy**, which can be negative.
A one-dimensional Gaussian has $\mathcal{H} = \tfrac12 \log(2\pi e \sigma^2)$,
which is negative when $\sigma \lt 0.242$.

## 7. Measuring distance between policies

### Kullback-Leibler divergence

The **Kullback-Leibler divergence** between two distributions is

$$
D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p \,\|\, q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim p}\left[\log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\right] \geq 0,
$$

with equality only when $p = q$, and is not symmetric.
Between policies it is computed per state and averaged over visited states: \
$\mathbb{E}_{s}\big[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_{\text{old}}(\cdot \mid s) \,\|\, \pi(\cdot \mid s)\big)\big]$.

### Trust region

A **trust region** is a neighborhood of the current policy, usually defined by a Kullback-Leibler divergence bound,
within which a local approximation of $J$ is accurate.
Limiting each update to this region prevents one bad step from destroying the policy.

## 8. Training vocabulary

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Rollout length** | Relevant for on-policy methods, its the number of steps $T$ collected per environment before an update. |
| **Batch** | All transitions used for one update, often $N$ parallel environments times $T$ steps. |
| **Minibatch** | A random subset of the batch used for one gradient step. |
| **Epoch** | One pass over the batch in minibatches. Proximal policy optimization runs several epochs on each batch. |
| **Update-to-data ratio** | Relevant for off-policy methods, its the gradient steps per environment step.  |
| **Warm-up** | Random actions before learning starts, so that the replay buffer is not filled by an untrained policy. |
| **Deterministic evaluation** | Evaluating with the mean action instead of a sample. It usually scores higher than the stochastic policy used for training. |
