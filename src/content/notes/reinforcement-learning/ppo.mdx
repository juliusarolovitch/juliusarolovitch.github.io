**Proximal policy optimization** (PPO) is the default on-policy actor-critic algorithm.
It collects a batch with the current policy, takes a few careful gradient steps on it, then throws it away.
It works for discrete and continuous actions and scales well with many parallel simulators.
Its off-policy counterpart is [soft actor-critic](/notes/reinforcement-learning/sac/).

Terms such as advantage, temporal-difference error,
on-policy and entropy are defined in [useful definitions](/notes/reinforcement-learning/useful-definitions/).
We use the same Markov decision process notation as in [reward functions](/notes/reinforcement-learning/reward-functions/): states $s_t$,
actions $a_t$, rewards $R_t$, discount $\gamma$, and objective $J(\pi) = \mathbb{E}_\pi\big[\sum_t \gamma^t R_t\big]$.

## Notation

- $\pi_\theta(a \mid s)$ is the policy (the actor) with parameters $\theta$.
- $\theta_{\text{old}}$ are the parameters that collected the current batch.
  They stay fixed while we update $\theta$.
- $V_\phi(s)$ is the value network (the critic) with parameters $\phi$.
- $\hat A_t$ is an estimate of the advantage $A^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}(s_t, a_t)$.
  We build it later in this section.
- $\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t[\cdot]$ means the average over all time steps in the batch.

## Start from the policy gradient

The policy gradient estimate on a batch is

$$
\hat g = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, \hat A_t\big].
$$

It is the gradient of the policy gradient loss

$$
L^{\text{PG}}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[\log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, \hat A_t\big].
$$

Vanilla policy gradient takes **one** step along $\hat g$ and then collects new data.
This is wasteful: we paid for thousands of environment steps and used them for one update.
The obvious idea is to take many gradient steps on $L^{\text{PG}}$ with the same batch.

That does not work.
$L^{\text{PG}}$ has the correct gradient only at $\theta = \theta_{\text{old}}$,
because the data came from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.
Further from $\theta_{\text{old}}$ it is not an estimate of anything useful.
Worse, for a sample with $\hat A_t \lt 0$ the loss keeps rewarding us for driving $\log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$ towards $-\infty$.
Repeated steps can destroy the policy.

We need an objective that (1) says how much a **new** policy improves on the old one,
(2) can be estimated from the **old** policy's data, and (3) stops being trusted when the new policy moves too far.

## How much does a new policy improve?

Take any two policies $\pi$ and $\pi'$. The **performance difference lemma** states

$$
J(\pi') - J(\pi) = \frac{1}{1-\gamma}\, \mathbb{E}_{s \sim d^{\pi'}}\, \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi'(\cdot \mid s)}\big[A^\pi(s, a)\big],
$$

where $d^{\pi'}$ is the discounted state-visitation distribution of $\pi'$.

To see why, run $\pi'$ and add up the old policy's advantages along its trajectory.
Write each advantage as $A^\pi(s_t, a_t) = \mathbb{E}\big[R_t + \gamma V^\pi(s_{t+1}) - V^\pi(s_t)\big]$.
The sum telescopes:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi'}\left[\sum_t \gamma^t A^\pi(s_t, a_t)\right]
= \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi'}\left[\sum_t \gamma^t R_t - V^\pi(s_0)\right]
= J(\pi') - J(\pi).
$$

Every $\gamma^{t+1} V^\pi(s_{t+1})$ cancels the $-\gamma^{t+1} V^\pi(s_{t+1})$ of the next step, leaving only $-V^\pi(s_0)$.
Its expectation over $s_0 \sim \rho_0$ is $J(\pi)$.
Rewriting the sum over time as an average over $d^{\pi'}$ gives the factor $1/(1-\gamma)$.

So $\pi'$ is better than $\pi$ exactly when, on the states $\pi'$ visits,
it picks actions that $\pi$ thinks have positive advantage.

## The surrogate objective

The lemma needs states from $d^{\pi'}$ and actions from $\pi'$.
We only have data from $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$.
We make two changes.

**Actions**: importance sampling turns an average over $\pi_\theta$ into an average over $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ exactly:

$$
\mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_\theta}\big[A(s, a)\big] = \mathbb{E}_{a \sim \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}\left[\frac{\pi_\theta(a \mid s)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a \mid s)}\, A(s, a)\right].
$$

**States**: replace $d^{\pi_\theta}$ with $d^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}$.
This is **not** exact.
It is a good approximation only while the two policies are close, because then they visit similar states.

Define the **probability ratio**

$$
r_t(\theta) = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)}.
$$

At $\theta = \theta_{\text{old}}$, $r_t = 1$ for every sample.
Dropping the constant $1/(1-\gamma)$, the **surrogate objective** is

$$
L^{\text{CPI}}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[r_t(\theta)\, \hat A_t\big].
$$

(CPI stands for conservative policy iteration, where this objective first appeared.)
Check its gradient at the old parameters:

$$
\nabla_\theta L^{\text{CPI}}\big|_{\theta_{\text{old}}}
= \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\left[\frac{\nabla_\theta \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)}\, \hat A_t\right]_{\theta_{\text{old}}}
= \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)\, \hat A_t\big]_{\theta_{\text{old}}}
= \hat g.
$$

So $L^{\text{CPI}}$ matches the true improvement $J(\pi_\theta) - J(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}})$ to first order.
It satisfies requirements (1) and (2).
It does not satisfy (3): maximized without limits, it pushes $r_t$ to infinity for every positive-advantage sample.

**Trust region policy optimization** (TRPO) adds the missing limit as a hard constraint:

$$
\max_\theta\; L^{\text{CPI}}(\theta) \quad \text{subject to} \quad \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\Big[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot \mid s_t) \,\|\, \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)\big)\Big] \le \delta.
$$

This is theoretically well-founded, since the error of the state approximation is bounded by the Kullback-Leibler divergence.
But solving it needs second-order information (the curvature of the Kullback-Leibler divergence) and a line search.
Proximal policy optimization gets most of the benefit with plain gradient descent.

## The clipped objective

Proximal policy optimization replaces the constraint with a change to the objective itself.
Choose a **clip range** $\epsilon$, usually $0.2$.
Then

$$
L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\Big[\min\big(r_t(\theta)\, \hat A_t,\; \operatorname{clip}\big(r_t(\theta),\, 1-\epsilon,\, 1+\epsilon\big)\, \hat A_t\big)\Big],
$$

where $\operatorname{clip}(x, l, u)$ limits $x$ to the interval $[l, u]$.
Read it one sample at a time, split by the sign of the advantage.

**Positive advantage**, $\hat A_t \gt 0$.
We want to make $a_t$ more likely, so we want $r_t$ to grow.
The term simplifies to

$$
\min\big(r_t,\, 1+\epsilon\big)\, \hat A_t.
$$

Once $r_t \gt 1 + \epsilon$, the objective is flat and its gradient is zero.
There is no further reward for making this action more likely.
Below $1 - \epsilon$ the term is still $r_t \hat A_t$ and keeps its gradient,
so a step that went the wrong way can be undone.

**Negative advantage**, $\hat A_t \lt 0$. We want $r_t$ to shrink. The term simplifies to

$$
\max\big(r_t,\, 1-\epsilon\big)\, \hat A_t.
$$

Once $r_t \lt 1 - \epsilon$, the objective is flat.
Above $1 + \epsilon$ it is still $r_t \hat A_t$,
so a step that accidentally made a bad action more likely is still penalized.

The pattern: clipping removes the incentive to move further in the **improving** direction,
but never removes the penalty for moving in the **worsening** direction.
The $\min$ makes $L^{\text{CLIP}}$ a lower bound on $L^{\text{CPI}}$ for every sample - a pessimistic estimate that ignores gains claimed far from the data.

The figure plots one sample's term against $r_t$.
The dashed line is the unclipped $r_t \hat A_t$, the shaded band is $[1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon]$,
and the dot marks the chosen $r_t$.

Things to try:

- With $\hat A_t \gt 0$, move $r_t$ above $1 + \epsilon$.
  The curve is flat and the status reports zero gradient.
  Now move $r_t$ below $1 - \epsilon$: the curve follows the dashed line again.
- Make $\hat A_t$ negative.
  The flat part moves to the left of the band.
  Moving $r_t$ to the right of the band still costs objective.
- Shrink $\epsilon$.
  The band where this sample can change the policy narrows, giving smaller, more conservative updates.

Clipping limits each **sample's** incentive.
It does not bound the Kullback-Leibler divergence between policies.
A sample whose ratio already sits outside the band contributes no gradient,
but other samples still move the shared network weights,
and those weights can push the clipped sample's ratio even further out.
After many epochs, the real Kullback-Leibler can be much larger than $\epsilon$ suggests.
Practical implementations therefore monitor an approximate Kullback-Leibler and stop the epoch loop early if it grows too large (see diagnostics below).

## Estimating the advantage

The objective needs $\hat A_t$ for every sample.
We have the critic $V_\phi$, trained to approximate $V^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}$.
From the definitions page, the temporal-difference error

$$
\delta_t = R_t + \gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1}) - V_\phi(s_t)
$$

is a one-step advantage estimate. It has low variance, but it is biased wherever $V_\phi$ is wrong.

Using $k$ real rewards before bootstrapping gives the **$k$-step advantage estimate**.
It is a sum of temporal-difference errors, because the intermediate values telescope:

$$
\hat A_t^{(k)} = \sum_{l=0}^{k-1} \gamma^l \delta_{t+l}
= -V_\phi(s_t) + R_t + \gamma R_{t+1} + \cdots + \gamma^{k-1} R_{t+k-1} + \gamma^k V_\phi(s_{t+k}).
$$

Small $k$ means low variance and high bias.
Large $k$ means the reverse.
At $k = \infty$, it is the Monte Carlo return minus a baseline.

**Generalized advantage estimation** (GAE) does not pick one $k$.
It takes an exponentially weighted average over all of them with a parameter $\lambda \in [0, 1]$:

$$
\hat A_t^{\text{GAE}} = (1-\lambda)\Big(\hat A_t^{(1)} + \lambda \hat A_t^{(2)} + \lambda^2 \hat A_t^{(3)} + \cdots\Big).
$$

The weights $(1-\lambda)\lambda^{k-1}$ sum to one.
Now collect the coefficient of a single $\delta_{t+l}$.
It appears in every $\hat A^{(k)}$ with $k \gt l$, each time multiplied by $\gamma^l$, so its total weight is

$$
\gamma^l (1-\lambda) \sum_{k=l+1}^{\infty} \lambda^{k-1} = \gamma^l (1-\lambda) \frac{\lambda^l}{1-\lambda} = (\gamma\lambda)^l.
$$

This gives the form actually used:

$$
\boxed{\hat A_t^{\text{GAE}} = \sum_{l=0}^{\infty} (\gamma\lambda)^l\, \delta_{t+l}.}
$$

- $\lambda = 0$ gives $\hat A_t = \delta_t$: one-step temporal-difference estimate, low variance, high bias.
- $\lambda = 1$ gives $\hat A_t = \sum_l \gamma^l R_{t+l} - V_\phi(s_t)$:
  Monte Carlo, unbiased, high variance.
- $\lambda = 0.95$ is the common default.

$\lambda$ and $\gamma$ play different roles.
$\gamma$ is part of the problem: it defines which return we maximize.
$\lambda$ is only part of the estimator: it trades bias against variance.

The sum is computed backwards over the rollout with a one-line recursion:

$$
\hat A_t = \delta_t + \gamma\lambda\, \hat A_{t+1}.
$$

## Episode boundaries

A rollout of $T$ steps usually contains several episode ends, and stops in the middle of an episode.
Each case changes the recursion differently:

- **Termination** at step $t$: no future reward exists.
  Set $V(s_{t+1}) = 0$ inside $\delta_t$ and do not carry $\hat A_{t+1}$ backward across the boundary.
- **Truncation** at step $t$ (time limit): future reward exists, but it belongs to an episode we did not observe.
  Keep $\gamma V_\phi(s_{t+1})$ inside $\delta_t$, using the real final observation,
  but still do not carry $\hat A_{t+1}$ backward.
- **End of the rollout**: the same as truncation. Bootstrap with $V_\phi(s_T)$.

Treating truncation as termination tells the critic that the world ends at the time limit.
That is a common and silent bug.

## The critic and the value target

The critic is regressed onto the **$\lambda$-return**, which is the advantage plus the value it was measured against:

$$
\hat R_t = \hat A_t + V_{\phi_{\text{old}}}(s_t), \qquad
L^{\text{VF}}(\phi) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\Big[\big(V_\phi(s_t) - \hat R_t\big)^2\Big].
$$

VF stands for value function.
$\hat R_t$ is computed once, before the update, and held fixed during the epochs.

## The full loss

Proximal policy optimization minimizes

$$
L(\theta, \phi) = -L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) + c_1\, L^{\text{VF}}(\phi) - c_2\, \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\Big[\mathcal{H}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid s_t)\big)\Big].
$$

The first term improves the policy.
The second trains the critic, with $c_1 \approx 0.5$.
The third is an entropy bonus with $c_2$ between $0$ and $0.01$.
It slows the collapse of the policy to a deterministic one.
It is a fixed-weight regularizer, unlike soft actor-critic, where entropy is part of the objective.

Two practical details:

- **Advantage normalization**: in each minibatch, subtract the mean of $\hat A_t$ and divide by its standard deviation.
  This makes the step size independent of the reward scale.
- **Actor and critic**: they can share a network body or be separate networks.
  Separate networks are more common for continuous control,
  because the scale of the value loss can disturb a shared body.

## The policy distribution

For **discrete actions**, the network outputs one logit per action and $\pi_\theta$ is the softmax over them.

For **continuous actions**, the policy is a diagonal Gaussian.
The network outputs the mean $\mu_\theta(s) \in \mathbb{R}^m$.
The standard deviations $\sigma \in \mathbb{R}^m$ are usually a separate learned parameter that does not depend on $s$,
stored as $\log \sigma$ so that it is always positive.
The log-probability is a sum over action dimensions:

$$
\log \pi_\theta(a \mid s) = -\sum_{i=1}^{m} \left[\frac{\big(a_i - \mu_{\theta,i}(s)\big)^2}{2\sigma_i^2} + \log \sigma_i + \tfrac12 \log 2\pi\right].
$$

If the environment has action bounds, clip the sampled action **only when sending it to the environment**.
Store and evaluate the unclipped sample,
so that $\log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ and $\log \pi_\theta$ refer to the same action.

## The algorithm

Run $N$ environments in parallel. Repeat:

1. **Collect.** Run $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ for $T$ steps in each environment.
   Store $s_t$, $a_t$, $R_t$, termination and truncation flags, and $\log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)$.
   This gives a batch of $NT$ transitions.
2. **Estimate.** Evaluate $V_\phi$ on all stored states.
   Compute $\delta_t$, then $\hat A_t$ backwards with generalized advantage estimation,
   then $\hat R_t = \hat A_t + V_\phi(s_t)$.
3. **Optimize.** For $K$ epochs: shuffle the batch, split it into minibatches,
   and take one Adam step on $L(\theta, \phi)$ per minibatch.
   Recompute $r_t(\theta) = \exp\big(\log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t) - \log \pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t \mid s_t)\big)$ on every step, since $\theta$ changes.
4. **Discard.** Set $\theta_{\text{old}} \leftarrow \theta$ and throw away the batch.

Step 4 is what makes proximal policy optimization on-policy.
After the update, the batch's states come from the wrong distribution, and the surrogate would trust them less and less.

The advantage computation and loss in PyTorch, with `[T, N]` tensors for the rollout:

```python
def gae(rewards, values, next_values, terminated, done, gamma=0.99, lam=0.95):
    adv = torch.zeros_like(rewards)
    last = torch.zeros_like(rewards[0])
    for t in reversed(range(rewards.shape[0])):
        delta = rewards[t] + gamma * (1 - terminated[t]) * next_values[t] - values[t]
        last = delta + gamma * lam * (1 - done[t]) * last
        adv[t] = last
    return adv, adv + values


def ppo_loss(dist, value, act, logp_old, adv, ret, eps=0.2, c1=0.5, c2=0.0):
    ratio = torch.exp(dist.log_prob(act) - logp_old)
    adv = (adv - adv.mean()) / (adv.std() + 1e-8)
    policy_loss = -torch.min(ratio * adv, ratio.clamp(1 - eps, 1 + eps) * adv).mean()
    value_loss = (value - ret).pow(2).mean()
    return policy_loss + c1 * value_loss - c2 * dist.entropy().mean()
```

Here `done` is termination **or** truncation, and `next_values[t]` is $V_\phi$ of the real next observation,
even when the episode was truncated and the environment has already reset.
`dist` is the policy distribution on the minibatch, with a `log_prob` that already sums over action dimensions.

## Diagnostics

Log these on every update:

- **Approximate Kullback-Leibler divergence**:
  $\hat{\mathbb{E}}_t\big[(r_t - 1) - \log r_t\big]$.
  This is a non-negative, low-variance estimate of $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}} \,\|\, \pi_\theta)$.
  Typical values are around $0.01$.
  Many implementations stop the epoch loop when it passes a threshold such as $0.015$ to $0.03$.
- **Clip fraction**: the fraction of samples with $|r_t - 1| \gt \epsilon$.
  Typical values are $0.05$ to $0.2$.
  Near zero means the steps are too small.
  Large values mean the policy has moved far from the data that measured its advantages.
- **Explained variance** of the critic:
  $1 - \operatorname{Var}(\hat R_t - V_\phi(s_t)) / \operatorname{Var}(\hat R_t)$.
  Near $1$ is good.
  Near $0$ or negative means the advantages are mostly noise.
- **Policy entropy** or $\log \sigma$: if it collapses early, the policy stops exploring.

## Typical hyperparameters

| Parameter | Typical value |
| --- | --- |
| Clip range $\epsilon$ | $0.2$ |
| Discount $\gamma$ | $0.99$ |
| Generalized advantage estimation $\lambda$ | $0.95$ |
| Steps per batch $NT$ | $2048$ (one environment) to $10^5$ or more (GPU simulators) |
| Epochs $K$ | $3$ to $10$ |
| Minibatches per epoch | $4$ to $32$ |
| Learning rate | $3 \times 10^{-4}$, often annealed linearly to zero |
| Value coefficient $c_1$ | $0.5$ |
| Entropy coefficient $c_2$ | $0$ (continuous) to $0.01$ (discrete) |
| Gradient norm clip | $0.5$ |
