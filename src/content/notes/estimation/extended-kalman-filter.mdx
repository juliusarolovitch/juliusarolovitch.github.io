The [Kalman filter](/notes/estimation/kalman-filter/) predicts a state, predicts what a sensor should see, and corrects the state when the reading is surprising. Its linear form assumes that motion and measurements are weighted sums of the state variables.

A range sensor does not measure a weighted sum of Cartesian coordinates. A rotating robot does not move as a weighted sum of its heading and speed. The **extended Kalman filter**, or EKF, keeps the nonlinear functions for predicting the state and measurement, and uses their local slopes to propagate uncertainty and calculate a correction.

## One state, a curved measurement

Imagine a cart on a straight track. A range sensor sits $b=2\,\mathrm m$ to the side of the track, beside its origin. If the cart is at position $p$, the distance to the sensor is

$$
z=h(p)+v,\qquad h(p)=\sqrt{p^2+b^2},
$$

where the range noise $v$ has variance $R$. Even though there is only one state, this measurement model is nonlinear.

Suppose our prior is $\hat p^-=3\,\mathrm m$ with variance $P^-=1\,\mathrm m^2$, and the sensor reports $z=4\,\mathrm m$. The predicted reading is $h(3)=\sqrt{13}\approx3.606\,\mathrm m$, so the innovation is $r\approx0.394\,\mathrm m$.

That is a discrepancy in *range*, not position. How much should we change $p$?

## Use the local slope

Near our current estimate, approximate the curved sensor function with its tangent:

$$
h(p)\approx h(\hat p^-)+H(p-\hat p^-),\qquad
H=h'(\hat p^-)=\frac{\hat p^-}{\sqrt{(\hat p^-)^2+b^2}}.
$$

$H$ says how much the range would change for a small change in position. At $p=3$, it is approximately $0.832$. One extra meter along the track produces about $0.832$ extra meters of range locally.

The corresponding Kalman update is

$$
S=H^2P^-+R,\qquad K=\frac{P^-H}{S},\qquad
\hat p^+=\hat p^-+K\bigl(z-h(\hat p^-)\bigr).
$$

With $R=0.25\,\mathrm m^2$,

$$
S\approx0.942,\qquad K\approx0.883,\qquad
\hat p^+\approx3.348\,\mathrm m,\qquad P^+\approx0.265\,\mathrm m^2.
$$

Here $P^+=(1-KH)P^-$ is the short scalar covariance update, and describes the local approximation. A gain above one is possible in other configurations: the gain converts a measurement residual into a state correction, so it is not generally a mixing fraction between two readings of the same quantity.

> **The function and its derivative have different jobs.** Predict the range with $h(\hat p^-)$, not $H\hat p^-$. Use $H$ to describe how errors near that prediction affect the range. The tangent includes an offset, which disappears only when we write it in terms of small deviations.

Things to try below:

- Move the prior center along the track. Watch the tangent's slope and the resulting gain change.
- Widen the prior standard deviation. The shaded horizontal span marks $\hat p^-\pm2\sigma$; the curve and tangent can disagree substantially within that span.
- Move the prior center to zero. The tangent is flat, and the EKF makes no correction even when the reading disagrees with its prediction.

### A useful failure case

At $p=0$, the range derivative is zero. The EKF sees no first-order information about position and sets $K=0$. But the real measurement still contains information: a range much larger than $b$ suggests that the cart is far from the origin.

The problem is symmetry. Positions $+p$ and $-p$ give the same range. A measurement can produce two plausible position regions, which a single Gaussian cannot represent well. Starting the filter on the wrong side of the sensor may keep it on the wrong side. A wider uncertainty band does not fix that representation.

The EKF works best when the current estimate is already near the right solution and the functions are nearly linear over the likely errors. Initialization, additional sensors, or a representation that can retain several hypotheses may matter more than tuning $Q$ and $R$.

## Nonlinear motion too

Now let the state evolve through a nonlinear function:

$$
x_k=f(x_{k-1},u_k)+w_k,\qquad z_k=h(x_k)+v_k.
$$

For the scalar case, predict the mean using the actual function and propagate its variance using the derivative:

$$
\hat x_k^-=f(\hat x_{k-1}^+,u_k),\qquad
F_k=\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_{\hat x_{k-1}^+,u_k},\qquad
P_k^-=F_k^2P_{k-1}^++Q_k.
$$

A local slope of two doubles a small state error, so it multiplies the variance by four. The process variance $Q_k$ accounts for additional uncertainty in the transition. As before, the variance rule is local; generally $\mathbb E[f(x)]\ne f(\mathbb E[x])$.

For a small-angle pendulum we might have used linear dynamics. For a pendulum swinging through large angles, the true acceleration contains $\sin\theta$. The EKF can use $\sin\hat\theta$ in the prediction and $\cos\hat\theta$ in the derivative instead of pretending the small-angle approximation holds everywhere.

## From slopes to Jacobians

With several states, each output can depend on several inputs. A **Jacobian** collects all those local slopes. For $f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^n$ and $h:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^m$,

$$
F_{ij}=\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j},\qquad
H_{ij}=\frac{\partial h_i}{\partial x_j}.
$$

Each column asks: if we perturb one state a little, which predicted quantities move, and by how much?

Consider a robot moving in the plane, with known speed $s$ and turn rate $\omega$. Let its state be $x=[p_x,p_y,\theta]^\top$. A forward Euler motion model over a short interval is

$$
f(x,u)=\begin{bmatrix}
p_x+s\Delta t\cos\theta\\
p_y+s\Delta t\sin\theta\\
\theta+\omega\Delta t
\end{bmatrix},\qquad
F=\begin{bmatrix}
1&0&-s\Delta t\sin\theta\\
0&1&s\Delta t\cos\theta\\
0&0&1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The last column explains how heading error becomes position error. If the robot points along the $x$ axis, $\theta=0$, and moves one meter, a small heading error $\delta\theta$ produces approximately $\delta p_y=\delta\theta$ meters. A heading standard deviation of $0.1$ radians therefore contributes about $0.1$ meters of lateral position standard deviation, even if position was initially known exactly.

The covariance prediction $FPF^\top+Q$ performs this calculation in every state direction at once, keeping the resulting position/heading cross-covariance. Here $Q$ is already expressed as additive **state** noise covariance. If uncertainty instead enters the speed and turn-rate controls, propagate their covariance through the control Jacobian $G=\partial f/\partial u$ to obtain a contribution $G\Sigma_uG^\top$.

### A landmark measurement

Suppose the robot measures its range to a known landmark at $(\ell_x,\ell_y)$. Define $d_x=p_x-\ell_x$, $d_y=p_y-\ell_y$, and $r=\sqrt{d_x^2+d_y^2}$. Then

$$
h(x)=r,\qquad H=\begin{bmatrix}d_x/r&d_y/r&0\end{bmatrix},\quad r>0.
$$

$H$ projects position errors onto the radial direction from the landmark. A small motion perpendicular to that direction barely changes range. One range reading cannot determine both position coordinates uniquely.

For a prior at $(3,4)$ relative to the landmark, $h=5$ and $H=[0.6,0.8,0]$. If $P^-=\operatorname{diag}(1,1,0.01)$ and $R=0.25$, then $S=1.25$ and

$$
K=\begin{bmatrix}0.48\\0.64\\0\end{bmatrix}.
$$

A measured range of $6$ moves the position estimate to $(3.48,4.64)$, outward along the radial direction. Its tangential position variance stays at $1\,\mathrm m^2$, while its radial variance falls to $0.2\,\mathrm m^2$. Heading is unchanged in this example because its prior cross-covariances are zero.

After motion has created position/heading covariance, a range residual can also correct heading even though the last entry of $H$ is zero. As in the linear cart example, the matrix gain uses relationships between state errors to update indirectly observed quantities. Such a correction uses prior relationships; it does not mean that one range measurement uniquely determines the whole robot pose.

## The EKF recursion

For additive, zero-mean, mutually independent process and measurement noise, the full algorithm is:

**Predict using the nonlinear model, then propagate uncertainty locally:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
\hat x_k^-&=f(\hat x_{k-1}^+,u_k),\\
F_k&=\left.\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\right|_{\hat x_{k-1}^+,u_k},\\
P_k^-&=F_kP_{k-1}^+F_k^\top+Q_k.
\end{aligned}
$$

**Predict the nonlinear measurement, then calculate a local correction:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
H_k&=\left.\frac{\partial h}{\partial x}\right|_{\hat x_k^-},\\
r_k&=z_k-h(\hat x_k^-),\\
S_k&=H_kP_k^-H_k^\top+R_k,\\
K_k&=P_k^-H_k^\top S_k^{-1},\\
\hat x_k^+&=\hat x_k^-+K_kr_k,\\
P_k^+&=(I-K_kH_k)P_k^-(I-K_kH_k)^\top+K_kR_kK_k^\top.
\end{aligned}
$$

Evaluate $F$ at the previous corrected state, and $H$ at the current predicted state. Both generally change at every sample. For linear $f(x,u)=Fx+Bu$ and $h(x)=Hx$, their Jacobians are the original matrices and this reduces to the ordinary Kalman filter.

## Practical checks

- **Check Jacobians numerically.** Compare each analytic column with $[f(x+\epsilon e_i)-f(x-\epsilon e_i)]/(2\epsilon)$ at several representative states. Choose perturbations appropriate to each state's units, and avoid discontinuities. Do the same for $h$.
- **Handle angle residuals as angles.** A bearing reading of $-179^\circ$ compared with a prediction of $179^\circ$ differs by $2^\circ$, not $-358^\circ$. Wrap the difference into a consistent interval, and normalize estimated headings as needed.
- **Keep timestamps and units consistent.** Integrate over the actual sample interval, express measurement covariance in the sensor's coordinates, and use radians in trigonometric derivatives. Non-additive noise needs its own Jacobians for covariance propagation.
- **Inspect uncertainty as well as the path.** A plausible trajectory can still have an overconfident covariance. Use the sensor experiments and innovation checks in the [Kalman filter note](/notes/estimation/kalman-filter/#finding-sensor-covariance-in-practice), remembering that EKF consistency checks are approximate.

The Luenberger observer chooses how a residual corrects a model. The Kalman filter calculates that correction using uncertainty. The EKF applies the same idea to local approximations of nonlinear models, with the accuracy and limitations of those approximations carried along.
